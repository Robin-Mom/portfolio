Aquaporins and Ions: A Matter of Thresholds

Part 1/3 — From Essential to Harmful: A Matter of Concentration

By Robin Mom · 3 min read

What makes the difference between an essential element and a poison? Sometimes, it is simply a matter of concentration.

Ions such as sodium, potassium, and chloride are fundamental to life. They contribute to cellular homeostasis, maintain membrane potentials, and support countless physiological processes essential to the survival of living organisms.

Yet, as with so many biological processes, balance is everything. Beyond certain thresholds, elements that are essential to life can become harmful.

During my PhD in bioinformatics, I investigated how ionic concentrations influence the molecular regulation of aquaporins (AQPs), a family of transmembrane proteins that facilitate water transport across biological membranes.

My research focused on the regulation of water fluxes in plants under drought conditions. At the cellular level, water scarcity can lead to changes in ionic concentrations and electrochemical gradients, creating conditions that may directly affect the function of membrane proteins.

To investigate these effects, we used an in silico approach based on molecular dynamics simulations.

Briefly, molecular dynamics allows us to reproduce the movements of atoms within a simulated molecular system. Each atom is represented explicitly, and its position evolves over time through the numerical integration of Newton's equations of motion. The interactions between atoms are described by mathematical potential functions collectively known as a force field.

This approach enables us to investigate molecular regulatory mechanisms at an atomic scale, revealing structural changes that would otherwise be difficult to observe.

In our case, we explored how changes in the ionic and electrostatic environment could influence aquaporin water permeability.

Our simulations revealed a fascinating mechanism: changes in electrostatic conditions could trigger a conformational rearrangement of a conserved arginine residue located at the narrowest region of the channel.

Under certain conditions, this arginine side chain shifted toward the pore lumen, creating a molecular barrier that restricted the passage of water molecules.

Even more interestingly, the sensitivity of this regulatory mechanism differed between aquaporins and appeared to correlate with the distribution of charged residues on their extracellular surfaces.

These observations suggested that the molecular composition of each channel could determine how it responds to ionic stress.

We published our findings:

Voltage-gating of aquaporins, a putative conserved safety mechanism during ionic stresses

This mechanism could contribute to protecting plant cells against excessive water loss under stressful environmental conditions. However, its physiological significance and the precise thresholds involved still require further experimental investigation.

And this brings us back to the original question.

Beyond a certain concentration, do ions become cellular poisons, or can they act as regulatory signals?

Perhaps both.

The answer may depend not only on the concentration of the ions themselves, but also on the molecular machinery of the cells exposed to them.

What appears harmful in one biological context might contribute to an adaptive response in another.

Ultimately, the distinction between essential and harmful is not always determined by the nature of a molecule alone. Sometimes, it emerges from the delicate balance between concentration, molecular interactions, and biological context.
