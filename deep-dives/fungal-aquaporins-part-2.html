Fungal Aquaporins: Variations on an Archetype

Part 2/2 — When the Archetype Breaks Its Own Rules

By Robin Mom · 5 min read

What happens when one of the most conserved amino acids in a protein family is replaced by another?

In the previous article, we explored the concept of functional archetypes through the molecular architecture of aquaporins, a family of transmembrane channels involved in transporting water and other small molecules.

We saw how certain structural features concentrate much of the relationship between protein sequence and biological function.

Today, I would like to take this reasoning one step further by examining an unusual substitution in one of the most characteristic regions of these proteins.

At the extracellular entrance of the aquaporin pore lies the aromatic/arginine selectivity filter, or ar/R constriction.

This narrow region plays a crucial role in determining which molecules can pass through the channel.

Four amino acid side chains contribute to its structural and physicochemical properties, and substitutions at these positions can significantly influence substrate selectivity.

Among these residues, one stands out for its remarkable evolutionary conservation: the arginine that gives the ar/R constriction its name.

Across a wide range of aquaporins, this positively charged residue helps maintain the electrostatic environment and structural properties required for selective transport.

Its conservation suggests that it performs a particularly important functional role.

Yet in Trichoderma atroviride, a microscopic fungus found in soils, we encountered a rather unexpected exception.

One of its aquaporin-related proteins, belonging to the X-intrinsic protein (XIP) subfamily, contains a lysine in place of this otherwise highly conserved arginine.

At first glance, the substitution seems relatively conservative.

Both arginine and lysine carry positively charged side chains under physiological conditions, suggesting that lysine might preserve at least part of the electrostatic contribution of the original residue.

But their chemical structures are not identical.

Arginine contains a guanidinium group, while lysine terminates in an amino group. These differences influence their geometry, hydrogen-bonding properties, and interactions with surrounding molecules.

And when a substitution occurs at one of the most functionally significant positions in a protein, even such subtle differences may matter.

So, could this unusual aquaporin still function as a water channel?

To investigate this question, we used all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, embedding the protein within a lipid bilayer and examining the behavior of water molecules inside its conducting pore.

The simulations revealed a continuous arrangement of water molecules within the channel, with water transport events observed over the simulated timescale.

Furthermore, the calculated water permeability was comparable to values obtained for other aquaporins.

Despite the unusual replacement of arginine by lysine, the channel therefore retained structural and dynamic properties consistent with an efficient water transport function.

But what about its biological role?

To explore this question, we also investigated T. atroviride mutants in which the gene encoding this particular XIP had been deleted.

The results revealed differences in mycelial growth and developmental characteristics, including effects on the formation of certain fungal survival structures.

However, the deletion was not lethal.

The fungus remained viable, suggesting that the loss of this protein could be tolerated, potentially through compensatory mechanisms involving other membrane channels or regulatory pathways.

Now, I realize that this might seem like a rather technical story.

After all, we are discussing the replacement of a single amino acid in a microscopic fungal protein.

But what fascinates me about this example is the broader paradox it reveals.

Aquaporins have been studied extensively for decades.

Their discovery is closely associated with Peter Agre, whose work identifying water channels in biological membranes was published in 1992 and earned him a share of the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Since then, thousands of scientific publications have explored their structures, functions, regulation, and physiological significance.

We now possess an extraordinary amount of information about this protein family.

And yet, there is still so much we do not understand.

Consider what we have just observed.

The substitution occurs in one of the best-characterized regions of the channel, a region that helps distinguish different functional subfamilies.

More importantly, it affects one of the most conserved amino acid positions within that region.

From the perspective of comparative genomics and natural selection, such strong conservation generally suggests significant functional constraints.

We might therefore expect a substitution at this position to have substantial consequences.

Yet the protein retains properties compatible with water transport, and the organism remains viable even when the corresponding gene is deleted.

Of course, this does not mean that the substitution has no functional consequences, nor that the protein itself is dispensable under every environmental condition.

The deletion mutants do exhibit phenotypic differences, and the precise functional implications of the arginine-to-lysine substitution remain to be established.

But that is exactly what makes this example so interesting.

A highly conserved structural feature suggests a fundamental functional constraint, yet biology reveals a degree of flexibility that our current understanding does not fully explain.

Perhaps compensatory mechanisms have evolved. Perhaps the lysine preserves the essential electrostatic properties of arginine while modifying other aspects of channel selectivity. Or perhaps the physiological significance of this structural variation lies in a function we have not yet identified.

Several explanations are possible.

And that is precisely the point.

The more deeply we investigate biological systems, the more we discover that even our most established principles leave room for unexpected complexity.

For me, this is one of the most fascinating aspects of scientific research.

Every new piece of knowledge helps us understand the world a little better, but it also reveals new questions that we had not previously imagined.

And perhaps the most important quality of a researcher is not the ability to eliminate uncertainty, but the willingness to explore it.

This brings me to the end of this first series of twelve scientific articles dedicated to my research.

Throughout this journey, we have explored molecular interactions, protein regulation, cellular homeostasis, physiological adaptation, and evolutionary conservation.

Yet beyond the diversity of these topics, one idea has gradually emerged.

Scientific knowledge does not bring us to the end of the unknown. It gives us new ways to approach it.

And so, I would like to conclude this series where every scientific investigation truly begins.

With a mystery.
