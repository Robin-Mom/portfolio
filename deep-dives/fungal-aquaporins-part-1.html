Fungal Aquaporins: Variations on an Archetype

Part 1/2 — How Small Structural Changes Shape Biological Function

By Robin Mom · 4 min read

How can a tiny change in a protein sequence lead to a major difference in biological function?

This is the question I would like to explore through the study of aquaporins, a family of transmembrane channels whose remarkable structural conservation conceals an equally remarkable functional diversity.

Aquaporins (AQPs) belong to the Major Intrinsic Protein (MIP) superfamily, an ancient and highly conserved family of membrane proteins found across almost all domains of life.

Despite their evolutionary diversity, these proteins share a characteristic molecular architecture that has remained remarkably stable over time.

Traditionally, aquaporins are divided into two major functional groups: strict aquaporins, which primarily facilitate water transport, and aquaglyceroporins, which can also transport small uncharged solutes such as glycerol.

But how can proteins sharing essentially the same structural framework perform different functions?

To understand this, we need to look more closely at their molecular architecture.

A typical aquaporin consists of six transmembrane alpha-helices surrounding a narrow conducting pore. Along this pore, backbone carbonyl groups provide sites for hydrogen bonding with water molecules, facilitating their passage through the membrane.

Two additional half-helices extend into the membrane from opposite sides and meet near the center of the channel. Each contains a highly conserved NPA (asparagine–proline–alanine) motif.

Together, these structural elements create a characteristic electrostatic environment that helps orient water molecules and contributes to the exclusion of protons, allowing water to pass without disrupting essential electrochemical gradients.

Intracellular and extracellular loops provide additional structural flexibility and can participate in the regulation of channel activity.

Yet perhaps the most fascinating feature lies near the extracellular entrance of the pore.

The archetype within the archetype: the aromatic/arginine selectivity filter, commonly known as the ar/R constriction.

This region forms one of the narrowest sections of the conducting channel and plays a central role in determining which molecules can pass through it.

Its structural and chemical properties are largely defined by four amino acid residues whose side chains shape the pore.

What makes this region particularly interesting is that even a small number of amino acid substitutions can significantly modify its geometry, hydrophobicity, and hydrogen-bonding properties.

A shorter side chain may widen the available space. A change in polarity may alter how a molecule interacts with the pore. And together, these relatively small changes can influence the channel's selectivity.

This is one of the molecular principles underlying the functional distinction between water-selective aquaporins and aquaglyceroporins, whose structural properties can accommodate larger solutes such as glycerol.

A few changes at precisely the right positions can transform what a protein is capable of doing.

To me, this illustrates a fundamental principle of molecular evolution: not all positions within a protein sequence contribute equally to its function.

Some regions tolerate considerable variability, while others concentrate a disproportionate share of the relationship between structure and function.

I like to think of these particularly informative structural features as functional archetypes.

They are the regions in which the connection between molecular architecture and biological activity becomes especially apparent.

From a more computational perspective, one could imagine describing these archetypes as a reduced-dimensional representation of protein function: a limited set of structural and physicochemical features that captures much of the information required to understand functional specialization.

Of course, this does not mean that the rest of the protein is irrelevant. Molecular function emerges from the entire structure and its environment. But certain features provide particularly informative starting points for understanding its diversity.

To illustrate this idea, I would like to share a study in which I participated, focusing on aquaporins from Trichoderma harzianum, a microscopic fungus commonly found in soils.

Fungi from the genus Trichoderma possess a diverse repertoire of membrane channels involved in the transport of water and potentially several small solutes.

In particular, some of their aquaglyceroporins display structural characteristics compatible with the transport of glycerol and other small polyols, molecules that can contribute to carbon metabolism and osmotic regulation.

Our research combined comparative genomics, phylogenetic analyses, structural modeling, and gene expression studies to investigate the diversity and potential functions of these channels.

We also explored how their expression changed during interactions between T. harzianum and Fusarium solani, a plant-pathogenic fungus.

The results suggested that aquaporin diversity could contribute to the remarkable capacity of Trichoderma species to adapt to different nutritional and ecological environments.

We published these findings:

MIP diversity from Trichoderma: Structural considerations and transcriptional modulation during mycoparasitic association with Fusarium solani olive trees

Although further functional investigations are required to determine the precise substrates transported by each channel, the structural diversity we observed provides valuable clues about their possible biological roles.

And this brings us back to the original question.

How much of a protein's functional identity is determined by a small number of critical structural features?

Comparative genomics often reveals highly conserved residues that appear to carry an especially important functional role.

Yet evolutionary conservation is not always synonymous with absolute structural rigidity.

Sometimes, even the most conserved features can vary.

And when they do, the consequences are not always what we would expect.

This is precisely what we encountered in another microscopic soil fungus, Trichoderma atroviride, where an unusual substitution challenged our understanding of one of the most characteristic features of the aquaporin family.
