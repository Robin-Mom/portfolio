Aquaporins and Ions: A Matter of Thresholds

Part 2/3 — When Colocalization Shapes Function

By Robin Mom · 3 min read

One of the fundamental principles of life is compartmentalization.

By separating biological environments, living organisms create the conditions necessary for specialized functions to emerge. These compartments develop distinct chemical properties, including highly specific ionic concentrations, which contribute to their physiological roles.

Consider the inner ear, for example.

Its endolymphatic compartment contains an unusually high concentration of potassium, which is essential for the electrical activity of sensory hair cells and the conversion of mechanical stimuli into electrochemical signals.

Another fascinating example can be found in the eye, where zinc plays important roles in several aspects of visual physiology, including vitamin A metabolism and the biochemical processes supporting vision.

But what happens to other proteins that happen to share these highly specialized environments?

In biology, molecular functions do not evolve in isolation. Proteins coexist within complex cellular environments, where their interactions and regulatory mechanisms may be shaped by the local conditions in which they operate.

This brings us back to aquaporins, the transmembrane water channels at the center of my research.

In the eye, aquaporin-0 (AQP0) is the predominant water channel of the lens. Interestingly, previous studies had shown that its water permeability could be modulated by zinc.

We decided to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation using molecular dynamics simulations.

Our results suggested that zinc could bind near a cysteine residue located at the interface between two adjacent AQP0 subunits.

This interaction appeared to influence the relative positioning of the subunits, triggering subtle structural rearrangements that increased water permeability through the channel.

What makes this mechanism particularly fascinating is its sensitivity.

At the atomic scale, even extremely small conformational changes can have substantial functional consequences. A slight repositioning of amino acid side chains within the conducting pore may be enough to modify the passage of water molecules.

Moreover, our simulations suggested that zinc does not regulate all aquaporins in the same way.

While zinc binding increased the water permeability of AQP0 and AQP5, it was associated with reduced permeability in AQP2 and AQP4 under the conditions investigated.

The same ion, therefore, could produce different functional outcomes depending on the molecular architecture of its target.

We published our findings:

Deciphering Molecular Mechanisms Involved in the Modulation of Human Aquaporins' Water Permeability by Zinc Cations: A Molecular Dynamics Approach

These results bring us back to the question of thresholds, but introduce another dimension: biological location.

The functional significance of an ion cannot be understood solely through its concentration. It also depends on where that ion is present, which proteins it encounters, and how those proteins are structured.

From an evolutionary perspective, this raises an interesting possibility.

In highly specialized tissues, molecular systems that coexist within the same environment may progressively develop complementary regulatory properties. A protein does not necessarily have to be the primary biological target of an ion to become sensitive to it.

Of course, our simulations do not demonstrate that such coevolution occurred in the case of AQP0 and zinc. They do, however, reveal a molecular mechanism consistent with the broader idea that local biochemical environments can influence protein function.

Perhaps, then, understanding biological regulation requires more than identifying the molecules involved. We must also understand the environments in which they meet.

What matters is not only how much of a molecule is present, but also where it is present, and what surrounds it.
