Plants and Uncertainty: A Matter of Timescales

Part 1/4 — Long-Term Adaptation: The Architecture of Water-Conducting Vessels

By Robin Mom · 4 min read

How do plants prepare for an uncertain future when they cannot simply move away from unfavorable conditions?

The 2023 synthesis report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) reminds us that climate change is already affecting food and water security across the world.

Beyond the overall increase in global temperatures, one of the greatest challenges lies in the growing intensity and frequency of extreme climatic events. Every additional increment of warming increases the risks associated with heatwaves, heavy precipitation, and other environmental disturbances.

For plants, which are deeply dependent on their immediate surroundings, these changes raise a fundamental question: how can organisms that are rooted in place adapt to increasingly unpredictable environmental conditions?

Understanding how plants integrate different timescales into their water management strategies is becoming essential for developing more resilient crops and protecting forest ecosystems.

During my Master's research in plant biology, I investigated the hydraulic properties of xylem vessels in Arabidopsis thaliana.

Xylem is the specialized vascular tissue responsible for transporting water from the roots toward the aerial parts of a plant. Its structural properties, including vessel diameter, geometry, and the characteristics of the connections between vessels, directly influence the efficiency and safety of water transport.

It was during this research that I became particularly interested in a phenomenon known as cavitation.

Under normal conditions, water moves through the xylem under tension, driven largely by transpiration from the leaves. However, when water becomes scarce, this tension can become sufficiently strong to trigger the formation or expansion of gas bubbles within the conducting vessels.

These bubbles can cause embolisms, interrupting water transport and potentially compromising the survival of the plant.

This creates a fundamental trade-off: plants must transport enough water to sustain growth while limiting the risk of hydraulic failure during drought.

A remarkable study published in Nature in 2012, entitled Global convergence in the vulnerability of forests to drought, revealed just how widespread this challenge is.

By comparing the hydraulic properties of hundreds of woody species across different forest ecosystems, the authors found that approximately 70% of the 226 species for which hydraulic safety margins could be calculated operated with relatively narrow margins against drought-induced damage.

Even more strikingly, these margins were largely independent of the average precipitation experienced by the different ecosystems.

In other words, trees growing in humid environments were not necessarily operating with substantially greater hydraulic safety margins than those living in much drier regions.

Global convergence in the vulnerability of forests to drought — Nature (2012)

To me, this convergence illustrates a fascinating aspect of plant adaptation.

Over evolutionary timescales, the hydraulic architecture of plants has been shaped by the competing demands of water transport efficiency, mechanical constraints, and resistance to drought-induced embolism.

In trees, new xylem tissues are produced throughout their development, allowing hydraulic architecture to reflect both inherited evolutionary traits and the environmental conditions experienced during growth.

This architecture represents a form of long-term adaptation: a structural framework within which the plant must manage its relationship with water.

But there is a problem.

What happens when environmental conditions change faster than the plant can modify its physical structure?

A tree cannot rebuild its entire vascular system every time a drought begins. It needs additional mechanisms capable of responding to environmental fluctuations on much shorter timescales.

This question became one of the motivations behind my PhD research, during which I investigated the molecular regulation of aquaporins and their contribution to leaf hydraulic conductance in poplar trees.

Aquaporins are transmembrane proteins that facilitate water transport across cell membranes. Unlike the relatively stable architecture of xylem vessels, their activity can be regulated dynamically in response to changes in the cellular environment.

And this opens up a completely different dimension of plant adaptation.

Long-term structural adaptation defines the possibilities. But survival also depends on the ability to respond to what is happening right now.

Perhaps the remarkable resilience of plants lies precisely in their capacity to connect these different timescales: the slow evolution of their physical structures and the rapid molecular responses that allow them to cope with an ever-changing environment.
