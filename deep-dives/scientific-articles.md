## Aquaporins and Steroids: A Matter of Perspective

### Part 1/3 — Exploring Ménière's Disease Treatments

**What could possibly connect an inner ear disorder, steroid hormones, and cellular homeostasis? Aquaporins (AQPs), of course!**

Well, I must admit that the connection wasn't always obvious to me either. Let me tell you how it all began.

After completing my PhD in bioinformatics, I was determined to continue the research I had started on the molecular regulation of a family of transmembrane proteins: aquaporins. There was just one problem: finding a postdoctoral position focused on this particular topic was far from easy.

Unless, of course, I could somehow secure the funding myself.

And so, with the support of a few fellow researchers, I continued running molecular dynamics simulations and looking for funding opportunities while unemployed.

Time went by, and despite my efforts, nothing seemed to work out.

Then, one day, my father, an ENT (Ear, Nose and Throat) specialist, told me about Ménière's disease, a condition he had been studying for many years.

Ménière's disease is an inner ear disorder that can cause episodes of vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, and other debilitating symptoms. Its underlying causes remain incompletely understood, although one of its characteristic features is an abnormal accumulation of endolymphatic fluid, known as *endolymphatic hydrops*.

One treatment used to relieve symptoms involves dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid. Yet its precise mechanism of action in Ménière's disease remains uncertain. Its therapeutic effects have traditionally been attributed, at least in part, to its well-known anti-inflammatory properties.

And that was when an idea emerged: **what if dexamethasone could interact directly with aquaporins?**

Aquaporins are membrane proteins found throughout living organisms. They form channels that facilitate water transport across cell membranes and contribute to the regulation of cellular water balance.

We began considering an alternative hypothesis: dexamethasone might directly influence transcellular water transport by modulating aquaporin activity, potentially affecting fluid homeostasis and pressure within the endolymphatic compartment.

To investigate this possibility, we turned to molecular dynamics simulations.

And, much to our excitement, the simulations supported our hypothesis! Dexamethasone appeared to interact directly with aquaporin-2 (AQP2), significantly reducing water permeability through the channel.

We published our findings:

[**A Perspective for Ménière's Disease: In Silico Investigations of Dexamethasone as a Direct Modulator of AQP2**](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom12040511)

Of course, an interaction observed through molecular simulations does not, by itself, establish a therapeutic mechanism in living tissues. But it provided a new molecular perspective on a longstanding clinical question.

Eventually, this publication attracted the interest of a cochlear implant manufacturer, opening the door to a postdoctoral research opportunity focused on the molecular regulation of aquaporins in the context of inner ear physiology.

**So, what did I learn from this experience?**

In scientific research, new possibilities often emerge from unexpected connections between disciplines. Sometimes, progress is not about looking harder in the same direction, but about discovering where else to look.

And in my case, it was quite literally by *listening* that I learned to shift my perspective.

### Part 2/3 — Exploring Inner Ear Physiology

**Could steroid hormones naturally produced by the human body directly influence inner ear homeostasis?**

This was the question we began exploring shortly after observing, through molecular dynamics simulations, a direct interaction between a synthetic glucocorticoid and aquaporin-2 (AQP2), a water channel associated with the regulation of fluid balance in tissues surrounding the endolymphatic compartment.

In the previous article, I described how bringing together two seemingly distant fields, otolaryngology and molecular dynamics simulations, had led us to investigate a new potential mechanism of action for dexamethasone.

But what if we took a step back and broadened our perspective?

What about the steroid molecules naturally present in inner ear tissues? Could they also regulate aquaporin activity? And might such interactions contribute directly to maintaining endolymphatic homeostasis?

Once again, we turned to molecular dynamics simulations to explore these questions.

This time, we investigated the interactions between AQP2 and ten steroid-related compounds: cortisol, Solu-Medrol, dexamethasone, methylprednisolone, cortisone, androsterone, progesterone, estradiol, cholecalciferol, and calcitriol.

By examining these interactions at the atomic scale, we were able to compare their binding behavior and their predicted effects on water transport through the channel.

**The most striking results involved vitamin D3.**

Both cholecalciferol, the precursor form of vitamin D3, and calcitriol, its biologically active hormonal form, exhibited particularly strong interactions with AQP2 and substantial inhibitory effects on water permeability in our simulations. Estradiol also emerged as a potent inhibitor.

These observations became even more intriguing when considered in a broader physiological context.

Previous studies had identified mechanisms involved in vitamin D uptake in epithelial cells associated with the endolymphatic compartment, where AQP2 expression had also been reported. Furthermore, several clinical studies had suggested associations between vitamin D deficiency and vestibular disorders, including vertigo.

Taken together, these observations led us to propose a new functional hypothesis: **steroid-related molecules might contribute to inner ear fluid homeostasis through direct interactions with aquaporin water channels.**

We published these findings:

**[In Silico Pharmacological Study of AQP2 Inhibition by Steroids Contextualized to Ménière's Disease Treatments](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2023.1270092)**

Naturally, an important distinction must be made here.

At this stage, our conclusions were based on *in silico* investigations. Molecular dynamics simulations provide valuable insight into molecular interactions, but like any scientific methodology, they involve assumptions, approximations, and limitations.

They cannot, on their own, demonstrate that the same mechanisms operate in living tissues or establish their physiological significance.

Further experimental investigations would therefore be necessary to validate the proposed regulatory mechanism.

Nevertheless, these results opened up a new way of looking at inner ear physiology.

What began as a question about the action of a therapeutic molecule had gradually evolved into a broader investigation of the possible roles of steroid-related compounds in cellular water regulation.

**The object of our research had remained the same. What changed was the perspective from which we approached it.**

### Part 3/3 — Exploring the Non-Genomic Effects of Steroids

**Could aquaporins, the transmembrane proteins responsible for facilitating water transport, also act as receptors mediating some of the non-genomic effects of steroids?**

At first glance, this might seem like a rather specialized question. Yet the non-genomic effects of steroid hormones have attracted considerable scientific interest, and understanding their underlying mechanisms could have important implications for the treatment of various diseases.

Interestingly, this was not the question we originally set out to investigate.

It was a question that found us while we were looking for something else.

In the previous two articles, I described how our investigations into the molecular regulation of aquaporins gradually led us to uncover potential functional interactions between these water channels and steroid molecules.

These findings initially inspired several research hypotheses concerning the pathophysiology of the inner ear.

But once again, we began to wonder: **what if we broadened our perspective even further?**

What if the molecular interactions we had observed were not restricted to inner ear physiology, but reflected a more general mechanism of steroid signaling?

This led us to propose aquaporins as potential membrane receptors involved in mediating the non-genomic effects of corticosteroids.

We presented this hypothesis in the following publication:

**[Cortisol Interaction with Aquaporin-2 Modulates Its Water Permeability: Perspectives for Non-Genomic Effects of Corticosteroids](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36675012/)**

To understand the implications of this hypothesis, we first need to distinguish between the genomic and non-genomic actions of steroid hormones.

Genomic effects involve changes in gene expression, typically mediated through intracellular receptors acting as transcriptional regulators.

Non-genomic effects, on the other hand, occur through mechanisms that do not directly depend on such transcriptional regulation. They can involve rapid signaling events, changes in membrane protein activity, and modifications of cellular physiology.

Our hypothesis was that **direct interactions between steroids and aquaporins could represent one such signaling mechanism, linking steroid binding to changes in cellular water homeostasis.**

In this context, the possibility that aquaporins could act as membrane receptors was particularly interesting.

This concept was not entirely without precedent, however. Other membrane channels, including members of the transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) family, have been investigated for their interactions with steroid-related molecules, and structural studies have revealed several examples of such interactions.

Our work suggested that aquaporins might represent another component of this broader picture.

Of course, identifying a potential binding site and observing changes in channel permeability through simulations does not establish that aquaporins function as steroid receptors in vivo. This remains a hypothesis requiring further experimental validation.

But for me, the broader lesson of this research journey extends beyond the molecular mechanisms themselves.

**What can we learn from the way this scientific question evolved?**

First, everything began with the original object of investigation: aquaporins. They provided the reference point through which we explored successive biological questions.

Second, the initial breakthrough emerged at the intersection of two seemingly unrelated fields: molecular dynamics and otolaryngology.

This reminds me of the ecological concept of *ecotones*: transitional zones between ecosystems, where environmental heterogeneity can create opportunities for increased biodiversity.

I believe something similar can happen in scientific research. At the boundaries between disciplines, different methods, concepts, and perspectives meet, creating fertile ground for new ideas.

Finally, throughout this journey, the central object of our research remained unchanged. What evolved was the context in which we examined it.

We began by investigating the molecular regulation of a water channel. We then explored its possible implications for Ménière's disease, extended our questions to inner ear physiology, and eventually considered a broader hypothesis concerning steroid signaling.

Each step opened the door to another.

**Sometimes, advancing a research question does not require changing what we study. It simply requires stepping back far enough to see the same subject in a wider context.**

## Aquaporins and Ions: A Matter of Thresholds

### Part 1/3 — From Essential to Harmful: A Matter of Concentration

**What makes the difference between an essential element and a poison? Sometimes, it is simply a matter of concentration.**

Ions such as sodium, potassium, and chloride are fundamental to life. They contribute to cellular homeostasis, maintain membrane potentials, and support countless physiological processes essential to the survival of living organisms.

Yet, as with so many biological processes, balance is everything. Beyond certain thresholds, elements that are essential to life can become harmful.

During my PhD in bioinformatics, I investigated how ionic concentrations influence the molecular regulation of aquaporins (AQPs), a family of transmembrane proteins that facilitate water transport across biological membranes.

My research focused on the regulation of water fluxes in plants under drought conditions. At the cellular level, water scarcity can lead to changes in ionic concentrations and electrochemical gradients, creating conditions that may directly affect the function of membrane proteins.

To investigate these effects, we used an *in silico* approach based on molecular dynamics simulations.

Briefly, molecular dynamics allows us to reproduce the movements of atoms within a simulated molecular system. Each atom is represented explicitly, and its position evolves over time through the numerical integration of Newton's equations of motion. The interactions between atoms are described by mathematical potential functions collectively known as a *force field*.

This approach enables us to investigate molecular regulatory mechanisms at an atomic scale, revealing structural changes that would otherwise be difficult to observe.

In our case, we explored how changes in the ionic and electrostatic environment could influence aquaporin water permeability.

Our simulations revealed a fascinating mechanism: **changes in electrostatic conditions could trigger a conformational rearrangement of a conserved arginine residue located at the narrowest region of the channel.**

Under certain conditions, this arginine side chain shifted toward the pore lumen, creating a molecular barrier that restricted the passage of water molecules.

Even more interestingly, the sensitivity of this regulatory mechanism differed between aquaporins and appeared to correlate with the distribution of charged residues on their extracellular surfaces.

These observations suggested that the molecular composition of each channel could determine how it responds to ionic stress.

We published our findings:

[**Voltage-gating of aquaporins, a putative conserved safety mechanism during ionic stresses**](https://doi.org/10.1002/1873-3468.13944)

This mechanism could contribute to protecting plant cells against excessive water loss under stressful environmental conditions. However, its physiological significance and the precise thresholds involved still require further experimental investigation.

And this brings us back to the original question.

**Beyond a certain concentration, do ions become cellular poisons, or can they act as regulatory signals?**

Perhaps both.

The answer may depend not only on the concentration of the ions themselves, but also on the molecular machinery of the cells exposed to them.

What appears harmful in one biological context might contribute to an adaptive response in another.

Ultimately, the distinction between essential and harmful is not always determined by the nature of a molecule alone. Sometimes, it emerges from the delicate balance between concentration, molecular interactions, and biological context.

### Part 2/3 — When Colocalization Shapes Function

**One of the fundamental principles of life is compartmentalization.**

By separating biological environments, living organisms create the conditions necessary for specialized functions to emerge. These compartments develop distinct chemical properties, including highly specific ionic concentrations, which contribute to their physiological roles.

Consider the inner ear, for example.

Its endolymphatic compartment contains an unusually high concentration of potassium, which is essential for the electrical activity of sensory hair cells and the conversion of mechanical stimuli into electrochemical signals.

Another fascinating example can be found in the eye, where zinc plays important roles in several aspects of visual physiology, including vitamin A metabolism and the biochemical processes supporting vision.

But what happens to other proteins that happen to share these highly specialized environments?

In biology, molecular functions do not evolve in isolation. Proteins coexist within complex cellular environments, where their interactions and regulatory mechanisms may be shaped by the local conditions in which they operate.

This brings us back to aquaporins, the transmembrane water channels at the center of my research.

In the eye, aquaporin-0 (AQP0) is the predominant water channel of the lens. Interestingly, previous studies had shown that its water permeability could be modulated by zinc.

We decided to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation using molecular dynamics simulations.

Our results suggested that zinc could bind near a cysteine residue located at the interface between two adjacent AQP0 subunits.

**This interaction appeared to influence the relative positioning of the subunits, triggering subtle structural rearrangements that increased water permeability through the channel.**

What makes this mechanism particularly fascinating is its sensitivity.

At the atomic scale, even extremely small conformational changes can have substantial functional consequences. A slight repositioning of amino acid side chains within the conducting pore may be enough to modify the passage of water molecules.

Moreover, our simulations suggested that zinc does not regulate all aquaporins in the same way.

While zinc binding increased the water permeability of AQP0 and AQP5, it was associated with reduced permeability in AQP2 and AQP4 under the conditions investigated.

The same ion, therefore, could produce different functional outcomes depending on the molecular architecture of its target.

We published our findings:

[**Deciphering Molecular Mechanisms Involved in the Modulation of Human Aquaporins' Water Permeability by Zinc Cations: A Molecular Dynamics Approach**](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25042267)

These results bring us back to the question of thresholds, but introduce another dimension: **biological location.**

The functional significance of an ion cannot be understood solely through its concentration. It also depends on where that ion is present, which proteins it encounters, and how those proteins are structured.

From an evolutionary perspective, this raises an interesting possibility.

In highly specialized tissues, molecular systems that coexist within the same environment may progressively develop complementary regulatory properties. A protein does not necessarily have to be the primary biological target of an ion to become sensitive to it.

Of course, our simulations do not demonstrate that such coevolution occurred in the case of AQP0 and zinc. They do, however, reveal a molecular mechanism consistent with the broader idea that local biochemical environments can influence protein function.

**Perhaps, then, understanding biological regulation requires more than identifying the molecules involved. We must also understand the environments in which they meet.**

What matters is not only how much of a molecule is present, but also where it is present, and what surrounds it.

### Part 3/3 — From Essential to Toxic: A Matter of Atomic Properties

**How can two chemical elements with seemingly similar properties play such dramatically different roles in living organisms?**

Cadmium has recently attracted considerable attention in France because of concerns over its presence in agricultural soils and food products, particularly cereals. One important source of contamination is the use of certain phosphate fertilizers containing this toxic metal.

Cadmium is a well-established environmental and public health concern. Prolonged exposure can lead to its accumulation in the body, with potentially serious consequences for human health.

But why is it so harmful?

Part of the answer lies in its chemical resemblance to two elements that are essential to life: calcium and zinc.

Like calcium and zinc, cadmium can exist as a divalent cation, carrying two positive electrical charges. This similarity allows it to interfere with biological processes that normally depend on these essential ions.

Yet the resemblance is far from perfect.

Cadmium differs from calcium and zinc in several atomic properties, including ionic radius, electronic structure, and polarizability. These differences influence how it interacts with biological molecules, while its similarities can enable it to bind to sites normally occupied by essential metals.

As a result, cadmium can disrupt molecular mechanisms that have evolved to depend on calcium or zinc.

Mercury provides another striking example.

Mercury ions have a particularly strong affinity for sulfur-containing groups, notably the thiol groups found in cysteine residues. This property allows mercury to interfere with the structure and function of numerous proteins, including certain aquaporins.

**It is fascinating that relatively subtle differences in atomic properties can separate an element essential to life from one capable of disrupting its fundamental mechanisms.**

I encountered this phenomenon once again through my research on the molecular regulation of aquaporins.

Over the years, numerous studies have revealed that aquaporins can interact with a wide variety of cations, including essential ions such as calcium and zinc, as well as toxic metals such as cadmium and mercury.

The mechanisms involved are remarkably diverse.

Some ions bind directly to specific amino acid residues, inducing conformational changes that affect water permeability. Others influence intracellular signaling pathways, protein trafficking, or channel regulation through more indirect processes.

In a review article, we brought together the available evidence to explore these interactions and identify common features among the different regulatory mechanisms.

[**Aquaporin Modulation by Cations, a Review**](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb46080470)

Working on this review made me reflect on a broader issue.

**Could the biological consequences of exposure to toxic metals extend beyond the regulatory pathways we currently recognize?**

I suspect they might.

The reason lies in the very nature of their mechanisms of action.

Calcium and zinc are involved in an extraordinary diversity of biological functions. Calcium participates in signaling pathways mediated by proteins such as calmodulin, while zinc is essential to the structure and activity of numerous proteins, including many transcription factors.

When toxic metals interfere with these regulatory networks, their effects may therefore propagate across multiple levels of biological organization.

Some of these interactions are already well characterized. Others may involve molecular mechanisms that remain poorly understood or have not yet been investigated.

The modulation of aquaporins by zinc provides one example of such a less familiar regulatory pathway.

This complexity makes the long-term consequences of chronic exposure to toxic metals particularly challenging to anticipate. Although toxicological studies can identify major risks and help establish exposure limits, predicting every possible interaction within a living organism remains a formidable challenge.

And this brings me to the final reflection of this series.

Throughout these three articles, we have explored how biological functions depend on concentration, location, and molecular properties.

An essential ion may become harmful when its concentration exceeds the capacity of a biological system to regulate it. The same ion may influence different proteins in different ways. And a toxic metal may disrupt essential physiological processes precisely because its chemical properties allow it to imitate other elements.

In each case, the boundary between function and dysfunction can be remarkably subtle.

**Perhaps this is the very nature of a poison: to resemble something essential closely enough to interfere with life, without ever truly taking its place.**

## Plants and Uncertainty: A Matter of Timescales

### Part 1/4 — Long-Term Adaptation: The Architecture of Water-Conducting Vessels

**How do plants prepare for an uncertain future when they cannot simply move away from unfavorable conditions?**

The 2023 synthesis report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) reminds us that climate change is already affecting food and water security across the world.

Beyond the overall increase in global temperatures, one of the greatest challenges lies in the growing intensity and frequency of extreme climatic events. Every additional increment of warming increases the risks associated with heatwaves, heavy precipitation, and other environmental disturbances.

For plants, which are deeply dependent on their immediate surroundings, these changes raise a fundamental question: how can organisms that are rooted in place adapt to increasingly unpredictable environmental conditions?

Understanding how plants integrate different timescales into their water management strategies is becoming essential for developing more resilient crops and protecting forest ecosystems.

During my Master's research in plant biology, I investigated the hydraulic properties of xylem vessels in *Arabidopsis thaliana*.

Xylem is the specialized vascular tissue responsible for transporting water from the roots toward the aerial parts of a plant. Its structural properties, including vessel diameter, geometry, and the characteristics of the connections between vessels, directly influence the efficiency and safety of water transport.

It was during this research that I became particularly interested in a phenomenon known as *cavitation*.

Under normal conditions, water moves through the xylem under tension, driven largely by transpiration from the leaves. However, when water becomes scarce, this tension can become sufficiently strong to trigger the formation or expansion of gas bubbles within the conducting vessels.

These bubbles can cause embolisms, interrupting water transport and potentially compromising the survival of the plant.

This creates a fundamental trade-off: **plants must transport enough water to sustain growth while limiting the risk of hydraulic failure during drought.**

A remarkable study published in *Nature* in 2012, entitled *Global convergence in the vulnerability of forests to drought*, revealed just how widespread this challenge is.

By comparing the hydraulic properties of hundreds of woody species across different forest ecosystems, the authors found that approximately 70% of the 226 species for which hydraulic safety margins could be calculated operated with relatively narrow margins against drought-induced damage.

Even more strikingly, these margins were largely independent of the average precipitation experienced by the different ecosystems.

In other words, trees growing in humid environments were not necessarily operating with substantially greater hydraulic safety margins than those living in much drier regions.

[**Global convergence in the vulnerability of forests to drought — Nature (2012)**](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11688)

To me, this convergence illustrates a fascinating aspect of plant adaptation.

Over evolutionary timescales, the hydraulic architecture of plants has been shaped by the competing demands of water transport efficiency, mechanical constraints, and resistance to drought-induced embolism.

In trees, new xylem tissues are produced throughout their development, allowing hydraulic architecture to reflect both inherited evolutionary traits and the environmental conditions experienced during growth.

This architecture represents a form of long-term adaptation: a structural framework within which the plant must manage its relationship with water.

But there is a problem.

**What happens when environmental conditions change faster than the plant can modify its physical structure?**

A tree cannot rebuild its entire vascular system every time a drought begins. It needs additional mechanisms capable of responding to environmental fluctuations on much shorter timescales.

This question became one of the motivations behind my PhD research, during which I investigated the molecular regulation of aquaporins and their contribution to leaf hydraulic conductance in poplar trees.

Aquaporins are transmembrane proteins that facilitate water transport across cell membranes. Unlike the relatively stable architecture of xylem vessels, their activity can be regulated dynamically in response to changes in the cellular environment.

And this opens up a completely different dimension of plant adaptation.

**Long-term structural adaptation defines the possibilities. But survival also depends on the ability to respond to what is happening right now.**

Perhaps the remarkable resilience of plants lies precisely in their capacity to connect these different timescales: the slow evolution of their physical structures and the rapid molecular responses that allow them to cope with an ever-changing environment.

### Part 2/4 — Immediate Adaptation: Sensing the Local Electrostatic Environment

**How can plants respond to environmental changes that occur far too quickly for their physical structures to adapt?**

In the previous article, we explored how the architecture of water-conducting vessels provides plants with a long-term strategy for managing their relationship with water.

But maintaining cellular integrity also requires responses to environmental disturbances occurring on much shorter timescales.

To understand how this is possible, we need to look at what happens at the molecular level.

During drought, a reduction in water availability can lead to changes in the concentration of dissolved ions and other osmolytes within and around plant cells.

These changes affect the water potential gradient across cellular membranes. If the extracellular environment becomes more concentrated in solutes than the cytoplasm, water may tend to move out of the cell through osmosis.

Without appropriate regulation, excessive water loss can compromise cellular integrity and ultimately threaten cell survival.

This is where aquaporins come into play.

Aquaporins (AQPs) are transmembrane proteins that facilitate water transport across biological membranes. By modulating their permeability, cells can regulate part of the water exchange occurring between their internal and external environments.

During my PhD in bioinformatics, we investigated whether variations in the local ionic environment could directly influence aquaporin activity.

To explore this possibility, we used molecular dynamics simulations, allowing us to investigate the interactions between ions, water molecules, and the protein at atomic resolution.

These simulations enabled us to characterize changes in the electrostatic environment surrounding the channel and examine how they influenced its molecular conformation and water permeability.

And the results revealed a particularly fascinating mechanism.

**Changes in the electrostatic environment could induce a rapid conformational rearrangement of an arginine residue located within the conducting pore, effectively restricting the passage of water molecules.**

Arginine carries a positively charged side chain. Its position within the channel makes it sensitive to the surrounding electrostatic environment.

When these conditions change, the interactions governing its conformation can shift, potentially repositioning its side chain toward the center of the pore.

The resulting molecular rearrangement can act as a gate, reducing the permeability of the channel.

We investigated this mechanism in the following publication:

[**Voltage-gating of aquaporins, a putative conserved safety mechanism during ionic stresses**](https://doi.org/10.1002/1873-3468.13944)

The simulations revealed conformational changes occurring on extremely short timescales, sometimes within just a few picoseconds.

Although these observations cannot establish the exact response time of aquaporins in living cells, they illustrate how rapidly molecular structures can respond to changes in their physical environment.

And this brings us back to the central question of this series: how do plants integrate different timescales to cope with uncertainty?

To move in a particular direction, a system first needs a long-term framework.

In the context of plant water management, that framework is partly provided by the architecture of the vascular system, whose physical properties reflect the evolutionary and developmental history of the organism.

But once this framework is established, adaptation also depends on what happens in the present moment.

As we move toward shorter timescales, we encounter mechanisms that respond directly to the immediate physical conditions surrounding a molecule.

At this level, there is no need for a new gene to be expressed or a new protein to be synthesized.

The existing molecular structure responds to its environment through the physical interactions that govern its conformation.

I find this particularly striking.

**An arginine side chain can behave almost like a microscopic compass needle, changing its orientation as the local electrostatic environment shifts.**

Of course, this is a metaphor rather than a description of biological perception. The response emerges from molecular forces, without any conscious detection or decision-making.

Yet the analogy captures something important: the ability of biological systems to respond to environmental changes is already embedded in the physical properties of their molecular components.

Perhaps, then, one of the secrets of biological adaptation lies in the integration of two complementary dimensions.

A long-term architecture that defines the possibilities.

And immediate molecular responses that allow organisms to navigate the fluctuations of the present.

**But between these two extremes, from evolutionary timescales to atomic movements, an entire world of regulatory mechanisms remains to be explored.**

### Part 3/4 — Intermediate Adaptation: Fine-Tuning Membrane Permeability

**Between the slow evolution of vascular structures and the almost instantaneous rearrangement of atoms, how do plants regulate their response to environmental stress?**

In the previous articles, we explored two very different timescales involved in plant water management.

At one extreme, the architecture of xylem vessels provides a relatively stable structural framework, shaped by development and evolution.

At the other, individual amino acid side chains can respond to changes in their electrostatic environment on extremely short timescales.

But biological adaptation is not limited to these two extremes.

Between them lies a remarkable diversity of regulatory mechanisms that allow cells to adjust their behavior within seconds or minutes, without necessarily modifying which genes are expressed.

Aquaporins provide a particularly interesting example.

Every cell possesses a specific combination of membrane lipids and transmembrane proteins, including aquaporins (AQPs), which facilitate water transport across biological membranes.

In plants, this protein family is especially diverse, reflecting the wide range of physiological environments and water-related challenges that plant cells must face.

Among these proteins, a subfamily known as plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs) possesses a particularly interesting structural feature: an intracellular region called *loop D*.

In some plant aquaporins, this loop acts as a molecular gate.

Depending on its conformation, it can either allow water molecules to pass through the channel or obstruct the intracellular entrance to the conducting pore.

This mechanism has been studied in the spinach aquaporin SoPIP2;1, whose molecular structure has been experimentally resolved in both open and closed conformations.

In the closed state, loop D interacts with neighboring regions of the protein, stabilizing a conformation in which hydrophobic amino acid side chains restrict access to the channel.

Changes in intracellular pH and the binding of divalent cations such as calcium have been proposed to influence the stability of this closed conformation.

**But how can the cell reverse this mechanism and open the channel when water transport is needed?**

One possible answer involves phosphorylation.

Phosphorylation is a common mechanism of cellular regulation in which a phosphate group is added to a protein, usually through the activity of a kinase.

This chemical modification changes the local distribution of electrical charges and can alter the interactions between amino acid residues, potentially modifying the protein's conformation and activity.

In aquaporins, phosphorylation can influence several aspects of regulation, including their subcellular localization and the opening or closing of the conducting pore.

During my postdoctoral research, we used molecular dynamics simulations to investigate how phosphorylation could control the conformation of loop D.

We focused on SoPIP2;1, examining the consequences of phosphorylating a specific serine residue, serine 188, located within this intracellular loop.

Our simulations revealed a fascinating sequence of molecular events.

**Phosphorylation of serine 188 promoted new electrostatic interactions with neighboring arginine residues, triggering a rearrangement of loop D that favored channel opening.**

Conversely, removing this phosphorylation shifted the conformational equilibrium toward less permeable states.

These results allowed us to propose a molecular mechanism linking a post-translational modification to the regulation of water permeability.

We published our findings:

[**Plant Aquaporin Gating Is Reversed by Phosphorylation on Intracellular Loop D—Evidence from Molecular Dynamics Simulations**](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms241813798)

Naturally, these simulations do not establish that the same mechanism operates identically in every plant aquaporin. The diversity of loop D sequences suggests that different members of the family may have evolved distinct regulatory mechanisms.

Nevertheless, they illustrate how cells can fine-tune the activity of proteins that are already present in their membranes.

And this is where the concept of timescales becomes particularly interesting.

Unlike transcriptional regulation, which involves modifying gene expression and potentially producing new proteins, these mechanisms act directly on existing molecular structures.

They belong to the broad category of *non-genomic responses*, allowing cells to rapidly integrate signals such as changes in calcium concentration, intracellular pH, and kinase activity.

Rather than rebuilding their membrane machinery, cells can adjust the activity of the channels already available.

**Aquaporins therefore act as molecular integration points, where different signaling pathways converge to regulate water transport.**

To me, this represents another remarkable dimension of plant adaptation.

The diversity of aquaporins provides a range of molecular possibilities, while regulatory mechanisms such as phosphorylation allow cells to select and adjust their functional properties according to their immediate needs.

The response is no longer limited to the instantaneous physical rearrangement of an individual amino acid side chain.

It becomes the result of coordinated signaling pathways acting upon existing membrane proteins.

**Between the stability of long-term structures and the immediacy of atomic interactions, living systems have developed an extraordinary capacity for adjustment.**

But what happens when environmental stress persists beyond the capacity of these regulatory mechanisms?

At some point, fine-tuning the existing machinery may no longer be sufficient.

The cell may need to change the machinery itself.

### Part 4/4 — Long-Term Cellular Adaptation: Reprogramming Membrane Composition

**What happens when environmental stress lasts too long for rapid molecular adjustments to be sufficient?**

Throughout this series, we have explored how plants integrate different timescales to cope with fluctuations in water availability.

We began with the architecture of water-conducting vessels, a relatively stable framework shaped over developmental and evolutionary timescales.

We then moved to the atomic scale, where changes in the electrostatic environment can trigger rapid conformational rearrangements within aquaporin water channels.

Finally, we explored intermediate regulatory mechanisms, such as phosphorylation, that allow cells to adjust the activity of proteins already present in their membranes.

But there is another level of adaptation.

**What if the cell needs to change not only how its membrane proteins function, but also which proteins are present in the first place?**

This is where gene expression comes into play.

When environmental stress persists, plants can reorganize their cellular machinery by modifying the expression of specific genes.

Rather than relying exclusively on the regulation of existing proteins, cells can alter their production of new proteins, changing the composition and functional properties of their membranes.

In the case of aquaporins (AQPs), this means changing the relative abundance of different water channel isoforms, each potentially possessing distinct transport properties and regulatory sensitivities.

This process involves transcriptional regulation: some genes become more actively expressed, while others are downregulated.

Unlike the rapid conformational changes described in the previous articles, transcriptional responses generally develop over longer timescales, ranging from minutes to hours, with subsequent changes in protein abundance potentially requiring even more time.

To investigate these responses, researchers can measure the abundance of messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules corresponding to different aquaporin genes.

During my research on poplar water physiology, I contributed to a study investigating how aquaporin expression and leaf hydraulic properties evolved under drought conditions.

We exposed poplar trees to controlled water stress and subsequently monitored their recovery following rewatering.

By combining ecophysiological measurements with molecular analyses, including quantitative PCR and tissue localization techniques, we investigated how different aquaporin families responded to changes in water availability.

Our study also examined the distribution of water within leaf tissues using X-ray microtomography, allowing us to observe the structural consequences of drought.

The results revealed an interesting contrast.

While water stress caused a substantial reduction in leaf hydraulic conductance and water content, some tissues surrounding the vascular system maintained a degree of hydration even under severe drought conditions.

These regions appeared to function as local water reservoirs, potentially buffering changes in water availability and facilitating recovery when favorable conditions returned.

Even more interestingly, these spatial differences in water distribution were associated with changes in the abundance and localization of specific aquaporins.

**This suggested that aquaporin regulation could contribute not only to controlling water transport, but also to redistributing water among different cellular compartments during drought and recovery.**

We published these findings:

[**Aquaporins and water control in drought-stressed poplar leaves: A glimpse into the extraxylem vascular territories**](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envexpbot.2018.12.016)

These observations highlight an additional dimension of plant adaptation: the ability to reorganize cellular functions in response to sustained environmental constraints.

Of course, the correlation between aquaporin expression and hydraulic behavior does not, by itself, establish a direct causal relationship for every channel involved. But it provides valuable evidence that changes in membrane protein composition are associated with the broader physiological response to drought.

And now, perhaps, we can bring the different pieces together.

Throughout these four articles, we have moved across several levels of biological organization, each operating on its own characteristic timescale.

First, **the structural architecture of water-conducting tissues** provides a long-term framework for water transport, reflecting developmental processes and evolutionary history.

Second, **the electrostatic sensitivity of individual amino acid residues** allows molecular structures to respond to local physical changes on extremely short timescales.

Third, **non-genomic regulatory pathways** can adjust the activity of membrane proteins already present within cells, integrating multiple signals over seconds or minutes.

And finally, **transcriptional regulation** can progressively modify the composition of the membrane protein machinery, supporting longer-lasting cellular adjustments.

These mechanisms do not necessarily operate in a strict sequence. They overlap, interact, and influence one another, forming a complex network of regulation.

Yet together, they reveal an essential feature of living systems.

**Adaptation is not a single response occurring at a single moment. It is the integration of multiple processes unfolding across different timescales.**

And this observation leads me to one final reflection.

What if we stepped back even further?

If we move beyond individual cells and organisms, into the world of genetic diversity and evolution, we encounter another set of processes through which biological information is preserved, modified, and transmitted.

The pangenome represents the genetic diversity found within a species, providing a broad reservoir of evolutionary possibilities.

The genotype represents the particular combination of genetic information carried by an individual, shaping its biological characteristics and potential responses to the environment.

Epigenetic mechanisms introduce another regulatory dimension, allowing gene activity to be modified without changing the underlying DNA sequence. Some epigenetic states can persist through cell divisions and, under particular circumstances, even across generations.

Finally, natural selection and evolutionary processes shape the distribution of genetic variants over successive generations, gradually transforming populations and, over longer periods, potentially giving rise to new species.

These processes are not direct equivalents of the four mechanisms of aquaporin regulation. Nevertheless, they invite a similar reflection on how biological systems integrate stability, variation, memory, and change.

At every level, living organisms inherit structures and information from the past while continuously interacting with the conditions of the present.

And at every level, their ability to respond depends on processes operating at different spatial and temporal scales.

**Perhaps this is one of the most fascinating characteristics of life: its capacity to connect the immediate moment with a history extending far beyond the individual organism.**

But how far can we follow this reasoning?

What happens if we move toward even larger scales, from populations to ecosystems, and from ecosystems to the evolution of the biosphere?

And what if we travel in the opposite direction, from proteins to atoms, and from atoms to the fundamental interactions governing matter?

How far can we go? All the way to infinity, perhaps?

Perhaps the boundaries are not as clear as we might imagine.

And perhaps understanding life means learning to navigate between these scales, discovering how each level both emerges from and influences the others.

## Fungal Aquaporins: Variations on an Archetype

### Part 1/2 — How Small Structural Changes Shape Biological Function

**How can a tiny change in a protein sequence lead to a major difference in biological function?**

This is the question I would like to explore through the study of aquaporins, a family of transmembrane channels whose remarkable structural conservation conceals an equally remarkable functional diversity.

Aquaporins (AQPs) belong to the Major Intrinsic Protein (MIP) superfamily, an ancient and highly conserved family of membrane proteins found across almost all domains of life.

Despite their evolutionary diversity, these proteins share a characteristic molecular architecture that has remained remarkably stable over time.

Traditionally, aquaporins are divided into two major functional groups: *strict aquaporins*, which primarily facilitate water transport, and *aquaglyceroporins*, which can also transport small uncharged solutes such as glycerol.

But how can proteins sharing essentially the same structural framework perform different functions?

To understand this, we need to look more closely at their molecular architecture.

A typical aquaporin consists of six transmembrane alpha-helices surrounding a narrow conducting pore. Along this pore, backbone carbonyl groups provide sites for hydrogen bonding with water molecules, facilitating their passage through the membrane.

Two additional half-helices extend into the membrane from opposite sides and meet near the center of the channel. Each contains a highly conserved NPA (asparagine–proline–alanine) motif.

Together, these structural elements create a characteristic electrostatic environment that helps orient water molecules and contributes to the exclusion of protons, allowing water to pass without disrupting essential electrochemical gradients.

Intracellular and extracellular loops provide additional structural flexibility and can participate in the regulation of channel activity.

Yet perhaps the most fascinating feature lies near the extracellular entrance of the pore.

**The archetype within the archetype: the aromatic/arginine selectivity filter, commonly known as the ar/R constriction.**

This region forms one of the narrowest sections of the conducting channel and plays a central role in determining which molecules can pass through it.

Its structural and chemical properties are largely defined by four amino acid residues whose side chains shape the pore.

What makes this region particularly interesting is that even a small number of amino acid substitutions can significantly modify its geometry, hydrophobicity, and hydrogen-bonding properties.

A shorter side chain may widen the available space. A change in polarity may alter how a molecule interacts with the pore. And together, these relatively small changes can influence the channel's selectivity.

This is one of the molecular principles underlying the functional distinction between water-selective aquaporins and aquaglyceroporins, whose structural properties can accommodate larger solutes such as glycerol.

**A few changes at precisely the right positions can transform what a protein is capable of doing.**

To me, this illustrates a fundamental principle of molecular evolution: not all positions within a protein sequence contribute equally to its function.

Some regions tolerate considerable variability, while others concentrate a disproportionate share of the relationship between structure and function.

I like to think of these particularly informative structural features as *functional archetypes*.

They are the regions in which the connection between molecular architecture and biological activity becomes especially apparent.

From a more computational perspective, one could imagine describing these archetypes as a reduced-dimensional representation of protein function: a limited set of structural and physicochemical features that captures much of the information required to understand functional specialization.

Of course, this does not mean that the rest of the protein is irrelevant. Molecular function emerges from the entire structure and its environment. But certain features provide particularly informative starting points for understanding its diversity.

To illustrate this idea, I would like to share a study in which I participated, focusing on aquaporins from *Trichoderma harzianum*, a microscopic fungus commonly found in soils.

Fungi from the genus *Trichoderma* possess a diverse repertoire of membrane channels involved in the transport of water and potentially several small solutes.

In particular, some of their aquaglyceroporins display structural characteristics compatible with the transport of glycerol and other small polyols, molecules that can contribute to carbon metabolism and osmotic regulation.

Our research combined comparative genomics, phylogenetic analyses, structural modeling, and gene expression studies to investigate the diversity and potential functions of these channels.

We also explored how their expression changed during interactions between *T. harzianum* and *Fusarium solani*, a plant-pathogenic fungus.

The results suggested that aquaporin diversity could contribute to the remarkable capacity of *Trichoderma* species to adapt to different nutritional and ecological environments.

We published these findings:

[**MIP diversity from Trichoderma: Structural considerations and transcriptional modulation during mycoparasitic association with Fusarium solani olive trees**](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0193760)

Although further functional investigations are required to determine the precise substrates transported by each channel, the structural diversity we observed provides valuable clues about their possible biological roles.

And this brings us back to the original question.

**How much of a protein's functional identity is determined by a small number of critical structural features?**

Comparative genomics often reveals highly conserved residues that appear to carry an especially important functional role.

Yet evolutionary conservation is not always synonymous with absolute structural rigidity.

Sometimes, even the most conserved features can vary.

And when they do, the consequences are not always what we would expect.

This is precisely what we encountered in another microscopic soil fungus, *Trichoderma atroviride*, where an unusual substitution challenged our understanding of one of the most characteristic features of the aquaporin family.

### Part 2/2 — When the Archetype Breaks Its Own Rules

**What happens when one of the most conserved amino acids in a protein family is replaced by another?**

In the previous article, we explored the concept of functional archetypes through the molecular architecture of aquaporins, a family of transmembrane channels involved in transporting water and other small molecules.

We saw how certain structural features concentrate much of the relationship between protein sequence and biological function.

Today, I would like to take this reasoning one step further by examining an unusual substitution in one of the most characteristic regions of these proteins.

At the extracellular entrance of the aquaporin pore lies the aromatic/arginine selectivity filter, or *ar/R constriction*.

This narrow region plays a crucial role in determining which molecules can pass through the channel.

Four amino acid side chains contribute to its structural and physicochemical properties, and substitutions at these positions can significantly influence substrate selectivity.

Among these residues, one stands out for its remarkable evolutionary conservation: **the arginine that gives the ar/R constriction its name.**

Across a wide range of aquaporins, this positively charged residue helps maintain the electrostatic environment and structural properties required for selective transport.

Its conservation suggests that it performs a particularly important functional role.

Yet in *Trichoderma atroviride*, a microscopic fungus found in soils, we encountered a rather unexpected exception.

One of its aquaporin-related proteins, belonging to the X-intrinsic protein (XIP) subfamily, contains a lysine in place of this otherwise highly conserved arginine.

At first glance, the substitution seems relatively conservative.

Both arginine and lysine carry positively charged side chains under physiological conditions, suggesting that lysine might preserve at least part of the electrostatic contribution of the original residue.

But their chemical structures are not identical.

Arginine contains a guanidinium group, while lysine terminates in an amino group. These differences influence their geometry, hydrogen-bonding properties, and interactions with surrounding molecules.

And when a substitution occurs at one of the most functionally significant positions in a protein, even such subtle differences may matter.

**So, could this unusual aquaporin still function as a water channel?**

To investigate this question, we used all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, embedding the protein within a lipid bilayer and examining the behavior of water molecules inside its conducting pore.

The simulations revealed a continuous arrangement of water molecules within the channel, with water transport events observed over the simulated timescale.

Furthermore, the calculated water permeability was comparable to values obtained for other aquaporins.

Despite the unusual replacement of arginine by lysine, the channel therefore retained structural and dynamic properties consistent with an efficient water transport function.

But what about its biological role?

To explore this question, we also investigated *T. atroviride* mutants in which the gene encoding this particular XIP had been deleted.

The results revealed differences in mycelial growth and developmental characteristics, including effects on the formation of certain fungal survival structures.

However, the deletion was not lethal.

The fungus remained viable, suggesting that the loss of this protein could be tolerated, potentially through compensatory mechanisms involving other membrane channels or regulatory pathways.

We published these findings:

[**Fungal X-Intrinsic Protein Aquaporin from Trichoderma atroviride: Structural and Functional Considerations**](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom11020338)

Now, I realize that this might seem like a rather technical story.

After all, we are discussing the replacement of a single amino acid in a microscopic fungal protein.

But what fascinates me about this example is the broader paradox it reveals.

Aquaporins have been studied extensively for decades.

Their discovery is closely associated with Peter Agre, whose work identifying water channels in biological membranes was published in 1992 and earned him a share of the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Since then, thousands of scientific publications have explored their structures, functions, regulation, and physiological significance.

We now possess an extraordinary amount of information about this protein family.

And yet, there is still so much we do not understand.

Consider what we have just observed.

The substitution occurs in one of the best-characterized regions of the channel, a region that helps distinguish different functional subfamilies.

More importantly, it affects one of the most conserved amino acid positions within that region.

From the perspective of comparative genomics and natural selection, such strong conservation generally suggests significant functional constraints.

We might therefore expect a substitution at this position to have substantial consequences.

Yet the protein retains properties compatible with water transport, and the organism remains viable even when the corresponding gene is deleted.

Of course, this does not mean that the substitution has no functional consequences, nor that the protein itself is dispensable under every environmental condition.

The deletion mutants do exhibit phenotypic differences, and the precise functional implications of the arginine-to-lysine substitution remain to be established.

But that is exactly what makes this example so interesting.

**A highly conserved structural feature suggests a fundamental functional constraint, yet biology reveals a degree of flexibility that our current understanding does not fully explain.**

Perhaps compensatory mechanisms have evolved. Perhaps the lysine preserves the essential electrostatic properties of arginine while modifying other aspects of channel selectivity. Or perhaps the physiological significance of this structural variation lies in a function we have not yet identified.

Several explanations are possible.

And that is precisely the point.

The more deeply we investigate biological systems, the more we discover that even our most established principles leave room for unexpected complexity.

For me, this is one of the most fascinating aspects of scientific research.

Every new piece of knowledge helps us understand the world a little better, but it also reveals new questions that we had not previously imagined.

And perhaps the most important quality of a researcher is not the ability to eliminate uncertainty, but the willingness to explore it.

This brings me to the end of this first series of twelve scientific articles dedicated to my research.

Throughout this journey, we have explored molecular interactions, protein regulation, cellular homeostasis, physiological adaptation, and evolutionary conservation.

Yet beyond the diversity of these topics, one idea has gradually emerged.

**Scientific knowledge does not bring us to the end of the unknown. It gives us new ways to approach it.**

And so, I would like to conclude this series where every scientific investigation truly begins.

With a mystery.
