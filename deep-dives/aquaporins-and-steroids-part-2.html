Aquaporins and Steroids: A Matter of Perspective

Part 2/3 — Exploring Inner Ear Physiology

By Robin Mom · 3 min read

Could steroid hormones naturally produced by the human body directly influence inner ear homeostasis?

This was the question we began exploring shortly after observing, through molecular dynamics simulations, a direct interaction between a synthetic glucocorticoid and aquaporin-2 (AQP2), a water channel associated with the regulation of fluid balance in tissues surrounding the endolymphatic compartment.

In the previous article, I described how bringing together two seemingly distant fields, otolaryngology and molecular dynamics simulations, had led us to investigate a new potential mechanism of action for dexamethasone.

But what if we took a step back and broadened our perspective?

What about the steroid molecules naturally present in inner ear tissues? Could they also regulate aquaporin activity? And might such interactions contribute directly to maintaining endolymphatic homeostasis?

Once again, we turned to molecular dynamics simulations to explore these questions.

This time, we investigated the interactions between AQP2 and ten steroid-related compounds: cortisol, Solu-Medrol, dexamethasone, methylprednisolone, cortisone, androsterone, progesterone, estradiol, cholecalciferol, and calcitriol.

By examining these interactions at the atomic scale, we were able to compare their binding behavior and their predicted effects on water transport through the channel.

The most striking results involved vitamin D3.

Both cholecalciferol, the precursor form of vitamin D3, and calcitriol, its biologically active hormonal form, exhibited particularly strong interactions with AQP2 and substantial inhibitory effects on water permeability in our simulations. Estradiol also emerged as a potent inhibitor.

These observations became even more intriguing when considered in a broader physiological context.

Previous studies had identified mechanisms involved in vitamin D uptake in epithelial cells associated with the endolymphatic compartment, where AQP2 expression had also been reported. Furthermore, several clinical studies had suggested associations between vitamin D deficiency and vestibular disorders, including vertigo.

Taken together, these observations led us to propose a new functional hypothesis: steroid-related molecules might contribute to inner ear fluid homeostasis through direct interactions with aquaporin water channels.

We published these findings:

In Silico Pharmacological Study of AQP2 Inhibition by Steroids Contextualized to Ménière's Disease Treatments

Naturally, an important distinction must be made here.

At this stage, our conclusions were based on in silico investigations. Molecular dynamics simulations provide valuable insight into molecular interactions, but like any scientific methodology, they involve assumptions, approximations, and limitations.

They cannot, on their own, demonstrate that the same mechanisms operate in living tissues or establish their physiological significance.

Further experimental investigations would therefore be necessary to validate the proposed regulatory mechanism.

Nevertheless, these results opened up a new way of looking at inner ear physiology.

What began as a question about the action of a therapeutic molecule had gradually evolved into a broader investigation of the possible roles of steroid-related compounds in cellular water regulation.

The object of our research had remained the same. What changed was the perspective from which we approached it.
