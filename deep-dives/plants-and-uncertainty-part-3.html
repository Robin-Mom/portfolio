Plants and Uncertainty: A Matter of Timescales

Part 3/4 — Intermediate Adaptation: Fine-Tuning Membrane Permeability

By Robin Mom · 4 min read

Between the slow evolution of vascular structures and the almost instantaneous rearrangement of atoms, how do plants regulate their response to environmental stress?

In the previous articles, we explored two very different timescales involved in plant water management.

At one extreme, the architecture of xylem vessels provides a relatively stable structural framework, shaped by development and evolution.

At the other, individual amino acid side chains can respond to changes in their electrostatic environment on extremely short timescales.

But biological adaptation is not limited to these two extremes.

Between them lies a remarkable diversity of regulatory mechanisms that allow cells to adjust their behavior within seconds or minutes, without necessarily modifying which genes are expressed.

Aquaporins provide a particularly interesting example.

Every cell possesses a specific combination of membrane lipids and transmembrane proteins, including aquaporins (AQPs), which facilitate water transport across biological membranes.

In plants, this protein family is especially diverse, reflecting the wide range of physiological environments and water-related challenges that plant cells must face.

Among these proteins, a subfamily known as plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs) possesses a particularly interesting structural feature: an intracellular region called loop D.

In some plant aquaporins, this loop acts as a molecular gate.

Depending on its conformation, it can either allow water molecules to pass through the channel or obstruct the intracellular entrance to the conducting pore.

This mechanism has been studied in the spinach aquaporin SoPIP2;1, whose molecular structure has been experimentally resolved in both open and closed conformations.

In the closed state, loop D interacts with neighboring regions of the protein, stabilizing a conformation in which hydrophobic amino acid side chains restrict access to the channel.

Changes in intracellular pH and the binding of divalent cations such as calcium have been proposed to influence the stability of this closed conformation.

But how can the cell reverse this mechanism and open the channel when water transport is needed?

One possible answer involves phosphorylation.

Phosphorylation is a common mechanism of cellular regulation in which a phosphate group is added to a protein, usually through the activity of a kinase.

This chemical modification changes the local distribution of electrical charges and can alter the interactions between amino acid residues, potentially modifying the protein's conformation and activity.

In aquaporins, phosphorylation can influence several aspects of regulation, including their subcellular localization and the opening or closing of the conducting pore.

During my postdoctoral research, we used molecular dynamics simulations to investigate how phosphorylation could control the conformation of loop D.

We focused on SoPIP2;1, examining the consequences of phosphorylating a specific serine residue, serine 188, located within this intracellular loop.

Our simulations revealed a fascinating sequence of molecular events.

Phosphorylation of serine 188 promoted new electrostatic interactions with neighboring arginine residues, triggering a rearrangement of loop D that favored channel opening.

Conversely, removing this phosphorylation shifted the conformational equilibrium toward less permeable states.

These results allowed us to propose a molecular mechanism linking a post-translational modification to the regulation of water permeability.

Naturally, these simulations do not establish that the same mechanism operates identically in every plant aquaporin. The diversity of loop D sequences suggests that different members of the family may have evolved distinct regulatory mechanisms.

Nevertheless, they illustrate how cells can fine-tune the activity of proteins that are already present in their membranes.

And this is where the concept of timescales becomes particularly interesting.

Unlike transcriptional regulation, which involves modifying gene expression and potentially producing new proteins, these mechanisms act directly on existing molecular structures.

They belong to the broad category of non-genomic responses, allowing cells to rapidly integrate signals such as changes in calcium concentration, intracellular pH, and kinase activity.

Rather than rebuilding their membrane machinery, cells can adjust the activity of the channels already available.

Aquaporins therefore act as molecular integration points, where different signaling pathways converge to regulate water transport.

To me, this represents another remarkable dimension of plant adaptation.

The diversity of aquaporins provides a range of molecular possibilities, while regulatory mechanisms such as phosphorylation allow cells to select and adjust their functional properties according to their immediate needs.

The response is no longer limited to the instantaneous physical rearrangement of an individual amino acid side chain.

It becomes the result of coordinated signaling pathways acting upon existing membrane proteins.

Between the stability of long-term structures and the immediacy of atomic interactions, living systems have developed an extraordinary capacity for adjustment.

But what happens when environmental stress persists beyond the capacity of these regulatory mechanisms?

At some point, fine-tuning the existing machinery may no longer be sufficient.

The cell may need to change the machinery itself.
