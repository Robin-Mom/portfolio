Aquaporins and Steroids: A Matter of Perspective

Part 3/3 — Exploring the Non-Genomic Effects of Steroids

By Robin Mom · 3 min read

Could aquaporins, the transmembrane proteins responsible for facilitating water transport, also act as receptors mediating some of the non-genomic effects of steroids?

At first glance, this might seem like a rather specialized question. Yet the non-genomic effects of steroid hormones have attracted considerable scientific interest, and understanding their underlying mechanisms could have important implications for the treatment of various diseases.

Interestingly, this was not the question we originally set out to investigate.

It was a question that found us while we were looking for something else.

In the previous two articles, I described how our investigations into the molecular regulation of aquaporins gradually led us to uncover potential functional interactions between these water channels and steroid molecules.

These findings initially inspired several research hypotheses concerning the pathophysiology of the inner ear.

But once again, we began to wonder: what if we broadened our perspective even further?

What if the molecular interactions we had observed were not restricted to inner ear physiology, but reflected a more general mechanism of steroid signaling?

This led us to propose aquaporins as potential membrane receptors involved in mediating the non-genomic effects of corticosteroids.

We presented this hypothesis in the following publication:

Cortisol Interaction with Aquaporin-2 Modulates Its Water Permeability: Perspectives for Non-Genomic Effects of Corticosteroids

To understand the implications of this hypothesis, we first need to distinguish between the genomic and non-genomic actions of steroid hormones.

Genomic effects involve changes in gene expression, typically mediated through intracellular receptors acting as transcriptional regulators.

Non-genomic effects, on the other hand, occur through mechanisms that do not directly depend on such transcriptional regulation. They can involve rapid signaling events, changes in membrane protein activity, and modifications of cellular physiology.

Our hypothesis was that direct interactions between steroids and aquaporins could represent one such signaling mechanism, linking steroid binding to changes in cellular water homeostasis.

In this context, the possibility that aquaporins could act as membrane receptors was particularly interesting.

This concept was not entirely without precedent, however. Other membrane channels, including members of the transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) family, have been investigated for their interactions with steroid-related molecules, and structural studies have revealed several examples of such interactions.

Our work suggested that aquaporins might represent another component of this broader picture.

Of course, identifying a potential binding site and observing changes in channel permeability through simulations does not establish that aquaporins function as steroid receptors in vivo. This remains a hypothesis requiring further experimental validation.

But for me, the broader lesson of this research journey extends beyond the molecular mechanisms themselves.

What can we learn from the way this scientific question evolved?

First, everything began with the original object of investigation: aquaporins. They provided the reference point through which we explored successive biological questions.

Second, the initial breakthrough emerged at the intersection of two seemingly unrelated fields: molecular dynamics and otolaryngology.

This reminds me of the ecological concept of ecotones: transitional zones between ecosystems, where environmental heterogeneity can create opportunities for increased biodiversity.

I believe something similar can happen in scientific research. At the boundaries between disciplines, different methods, concepts, and perspectives meet, creating fertile ground for new ideas.

Finally, throughout this journey, the central object of our research remained unchanged. What evolved was the context in which we examined it.

We began by investigating the molecular regulation of a water channel. We then explored its possible implications for Ménière's disease, extended our questions to inner ear physiology, and eventually considered a broader hypothesis concerning steroid signaling.

Each step opened the door to another.

Sometimes, advancing a research question does not require changing what we study. It simply requires stepping back far enough to see the same subject in a wider context.
