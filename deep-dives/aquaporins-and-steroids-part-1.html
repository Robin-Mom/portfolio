Aquaporins and Steroids: A Matter of Perspective

Part 1/3 — Exploring Ménière's Disease Treatments

By Robin Mom · 3 min read

What could possibly connect an inner ear disorder, steroid hormones, and cellular homeostasis? Aquaporins (AQPs), of course!

Well, I must admit that the connection wasn't always obvious to me either. Let me tell you how it all began.

After completing my PhD in bioinformatics, I was determined to continue the research I had started on the molecular regulation of a family of transmembrane proteins: aquaporins. There was just one problem: finding a postdoctoral position focused on this particular topic was far from easy.

Unless, of course, I could somehow secure the funding myself.

And so, with the support of a few fellow researchers, I continued running molecular dynamics simulations and looking for funding opportunities while unemployed.

Time went by, and despite my efforts, nothing seemed to work out.

Then, one day, my father, an ENT (Ear, Nose and Throat) specialist, told me about Ménière's disease, a condition he had been studying for many years.

Ménière's disease is an inner ear disorder that can cause episodes of vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, and other debilitating symptoms. Its underlying causes remain incompletely understood, although one of its characteristic features is an abnormal accumulation of endolymphatic fluid, known as endolymphatic hydrops.

One treatment used to relieve symptoms involves dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid. Yet its precise mechanism of action in Ménière's disease remains uncertain. Its therapeutic effects have traditionally been attributed, at least in part, to its well-known anti-inflammatory properties.

And that was when an idea emerged: what if dexamethasone could interact directly with aquaporins?

Aquaporins are membrane proteins found throughout living organisms. They form channels that facilitate water transport across cell membranes and contribute to the regulation of cellular water balance.

We began considering an alternative hypothesis: dexamethasone might directly influence transcellular water transport by modulating aquaporin activity, potentially affecting fluid homeostasis and pressure within the endolymphatic compartment.

To investigate this possibility, we turned to molecular dynamics simulations.

And, much to our excitement, the simulations supported our hypothesis! Dexamethasone appeared to interact directly with aquaporin-2 (AQP2), significantly reducing water permeability through the channel.

We published our findings:

A Perspective for Ménière's Disease: In Silico Investigations of Dexamethasone as a Direct Modulator of AQP2

Of course, an interaction observed through molecular simulations does not, by itself, establish a therapeutic mechanism in living tissues. But it provided a new molecular perspective on a longstanding clinical question.

Eventually, this publication attracted the interest of a cochlear implant manufacturer, opening the door to a postdoctoral research opportunity focused on the molecular regulation of aquaporins in the context of inner ear physiology.

So, what did I learn from this experience?

In scientific research, new possibilities often emerge from unexpected connections between disciplines. Sometimes, progress is not about looking harder in the same direction, but about discovering where else to look.

And in my case, it was quite literally by listening that I learned to shift my perspective.
